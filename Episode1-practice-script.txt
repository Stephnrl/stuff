SLIDE 3 - THE REAL PROBLEM
------------------------------------------------------
The work isn't slow. The handoffs are.

The people who know our security and compliance
standards sit on other teams, and each team has its own
queue. A question that takes them ten minutes to answer
can sit there for days. And by the time the feedback
comes back, I've moved on to something else and have to
switch back.

And this isn't about being new to the work. My day job
is hardening our AWS baseline and running our EKS
clusters. I still learned some of our standards only
when the review came back.

What I wish I'd known earlier: the standards existed. I
just didn't have them in front of me while I was doing
the work.

(pause) So I tried to fix that with AI. It didn't work
the first time.

------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 4 - THREE THINGS I TRIED
------------------------------------------------------
I tried three things.

First, a generic chatbot. I asked it for an IAM policy,
and it gave me one, very confidently. It was exactly the
kind of broad policy security sends back. That was my
mistake: I trusted an answer that didn't know our
standards.

Second, I pasted our standards into the chat. That was
better. But it came out different every time, and it was
gone the next session.

Third, I gave the agent a role. An agent that knows our
standards and has the skills to apply them. That's what
worked.

(pause) That third step has two pieces. First, skills.

------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 5 - SKILLS IN ONE MINUTE
------------------------------------------------------
A skill teaches an agent how we do a task.

It's just a folder with a Markdown file in it: a name, a
description of when to use it, and the steps. The agent
reads only the descriptions at first, and opens a skill
when a task matches, so it can carry a lot of them. And
it's an open standard, so the same skill works in
Copilot, VS Code, Cursor and others.

I won't go deeper today. Building your own skills is
Episode 2.

(pause) Skills solved the how. They didn't solve the who.

------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 6 - PERSONA
------------------------------------------------------
Here's the problem a persona solves: a generic AI
answers like nobody in particular.

When I need a security answer, I don't want a general
opinion. A generic assistant will answer anything. It
doesn't know where to stop. And it gives a developer and
an auditor the same answer.

(point at the right card) What I want is someone who
thinks like our security team. Someone who applies our
standards every time, knows their limits, and tells me
what still needs a person.

That's a persona. It's a role description, the same kind
you'd write for a new hire.

(pause)

Skills say how. A persona says who.

(pause) And when you put a persona and its skills
together, you get a kit.

------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 7 - KIT
------------------------------------------------------
A kit is a specialist, defined in one file.

(point at the right column) It says who the agent is,
which skills it can use, the team rules it always reads,
the only tools it's allowed to touch, and which model it
runs on.

Think of it as the job description plus the toolbox for
one hire. When I start an agent in sncfleet.ai, I pick a
kit, and that's who shows up.

And because it's one file, if I change the kit, every
agent I start after that gets the change.

(pause) So here's the team I've hired.

------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 8 - MEET MY TEAM
------------------------------------------------------
Meet my team.

Two of them do the hands-on work: the DevSecOps engineer
and the .NET developer. The other three give me a first
pass that I'd otherwise wait days for. The security
engineer, before the security review. The CMMC analyst,
before compliance. And the PR reviewer, before a
teammate looks at it.

And I want to be clear about this: they don't replace
those teams. They make sure that when my work reaches
those teams, it's already right.

(point at the dark card) And adding a new specialist
isn't building a new tool. It's a persona file and a
JSON file, reviewed like any other change.

(pause) So let's go back to that IAM ticket.

------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 9 - THE SAME TICKET
------------------------------------------------------
Same ticket. But this time, I had my security engineer.

I started an agent with the security-engineer kit and
told it what our developers needed to do. It drafted the
policy to our least-privilege standard. It explained
every permission, and it wrote the justification that
security would ask for. Then I reviewed it, adjusted
what I wanted, and submitted it in ServiceNow.

(pause)

About a day of work. One request. Zero revisions from
the security team.

(point at step 4) And notice who approved it: the
security team. The agent didn't skip the review. It got
the work right before it reached them.

(pause) And it isn't just approvals.

------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 10 - BOTTLEROCKET
------------------------------------------------------
It's the work itself, too.

We moved our EKS nodes to Bottlerocket, with FIPS.
Testing a new node OS normally takes weeks. Does FIPS
work? Do our workloads run on it? Does DNS resolve? Do
the logs arrive, and do they check out?

With my agents helping me plan the tests, read the
output and chase down the failures, it took about two
days.

(pause) Which raises a fair question: can we trust it?

------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 11 - GUARDRAILS
------------------------------------------------------
Agents prepare the work. People still approve it.

Agents can be wrong, and skills can run commands, so the
design assumes mistakes will happen.

(point at two cards) The two that matter most: the agent
never holds real credentials, and a person always
merges. Nothing it does reaches a live environment
without review.

The rest is here for you to read: it only gets the tools
its kit names, skills are reviewed like code, kits are
scanned for secrets, and there are guardrail hooks and
an audit trail.

(pause) So what does this change for us?

------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 12 - WHAT CHANGES FOR US
------------------------------------------------------
Four things.

First-pass approvals. Our work reaches security and
compliance already meeting our standards, like that IAM
request.

Days, not weeks. Like Bottlerocket: weeks of testing in
two days.

Auditable by default. Our procedures live in git, and
everything an agent does arrives as commits a person
reviews. That matters for CMMC.

And right-sized cost. Routine work runs on cheaper
models, and only the hard problems use the strongest
one.

policy to my security engineer, live.
